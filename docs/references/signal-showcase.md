# The Shape of a Signal: source pack

Source and asset reference for early telegraphy, the Morse–Vail key and communication networks. Source access checked 2026-11-23.

## Core institutional sources

| Source | Supported claims and limits |
| --- | --- |
| [Smithsonian: Morse–Vail Telegraph Key](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1096762) | Alfred Vail made this key as an improvement on Morse's transmitter. The museum says it is **believed** to be from the first Baltimore–Washington line; retain that qualification. | [U.S. Senate: First Telegraph Message, May 24, 1844](https://www.senate.gov/about/images/documents/telegraph-message-1844-loc.htm) | Morse sent **“What hath God wrought”** from the U.S. Capitol to Alfred Vail in Baltimore on 24 May 1844. | [Library of Congress: Invention of the Telegraph](https://www.loc.gov/collections/samuel-morse-papers/articles-and-essays/invention-of-the-telegraph/) | Gale helped Morse overcome distance problems using Henry's relay work; Vail also contributed. Congress funded the forty-mile demonstration line in 1843. Defective insulation frustrated the underground plan, and overhead wires completed the connection. Early receivers embossed paper tape; interpretation by sound developed later. Do not imply that every 1844 recipient heard an electronic tone. |
| [Ingenium: Telegraph key](https://ingenium.ca/en/learn/teachers-zone/resources/telegraph-key/) | A museum explanation of the simple circuit: pressing the key completes the circuit, allowing a receiving indicator to respond; releasing it breaks the circuit. Suitable support for a clearly labeled interactive teaching model. Its pictured Camelback key dates to 1848–69 and is not the local Smithsonian object. |
| [The Met: A Night Scene. The Cable Entangled and Nearly Broken, July 18th, 1866](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/364362) | The initially successful 1858 Atlantic cable failed after three weeks. Two working cables were established in July and September 1866. Success involved engineers, technicians, sailors, investors and help from the British and U.S. navies. Dudley's watercolor depicts danger during the 1866 expedition; its catalog date is 1865–66. |
| [National Park Service: French Transatlantic Cable](https://www.nps.gov/caco/learn/historyculture/french-transatlantic-cable.htm) | The 1858 connection soon failed; a new cable between Ireland and Newfoundland succeeded in 1866. Early Atlantic routes generally reached North America through Newfoundland or Nova Scotia and continued overland toward New York. This supports distinguishing a transatlantic segment from an entire end-to-end message network. |

## Narrative guardrails

- **The signal:** the key is a controllable electrical contact. A short or longer closure can stand for a code element; the shared code and timing make the elements interpretable. A visual circuit or pulse trace is an explanatory diagram, not a measurement of the preserved key. If the interface teaches present-day International Morse, label it as such rather than claiming to reproduce the exact American code sent in 1844.
- **The network:** longer connections needed more than an operator's hand: receiving equipment, batteries, wire, relay arrangements, people and maintenance all mattered. The LOC account supports discussing relays as a response to distance; do not draw a single uninterrupted “instant message” line around the world and call it the 1844 system.
- **The date:** 1844 is the Washington–Baltimore inauguration, 1858 is a short-lived Atlantic connection, and 1866 is the lasting Atlantic achievement. The key provides an entry into that later history; it is not evidence that this specific key crossed the Atlantic or operated the cable.
- **The institutions:** public funding and permissions helped build the American demonstration. The ocean connection required companies, capital, ships and coordinated work. **Interpretation:** a network's success depended on a maintained system as well as an ingenious instrument. The sources support that synthesis without claiming equal access or universal benefit.

## The surviving message is not one interchangeable artifact

The [Smithsonian's message record](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/object/nmah_713485) distinguishes three objects: the 24 May Washington-to-Baltimore original held by LOC; Vail's confirmation held by the Connecticut Historical Society; and the Smithsonian's **25 May 1844 souvenir**. Do not caption the Smithsonian souvenir as the first message itself. The Senate's LOC facsimile is a valid citation for the displayed words, but its JPEG is not a registered local asset. The exact quoted message is four words; punctuation and capitalization can vary between transcriptions.

## Map scope

The regional route should identify **Washington, D.C. → Baltimore**, with the U.S. Capitol and the B&O Pratt Street station identified in the Smithsonian message record. Use a schematic connection unless the exact historical wire alignment is separately sourced. Do not quietly substitute the surviving Mount Clare museum building for the reception point.

For the lasting Atlantic connection, identify **Valentia Island, Ireland → Heart's Content, Newfoundland**. [Parks Canada's designation](https://www.pc.gc.ca/apps/dfhd/page_nhs_eng.aspx?id=1950) confirms the 1866 landing at Heart's Content. Its wording should not erase the successful but short-lived 1858 crossing. [The provincial historic-site operator](https://www.seethesites.ca/sites/hearts-content-cable-station/) describes 1866 as the first successful permanent connection and warns that today's cable-station building dates to 1875, with a 1918 extension. The locality names are verified; no surveyed terminal coordinates or detailed cable track were verified for this pack. Any coarse geography in the teaching map must be presented as schematic.

## Source limits

The source packet establishes neither exact cable geometry nor exact 1844 code timing or complete historical coverage. Remote reference links are not runtime image registrations; only use local assets with recorded rights.
